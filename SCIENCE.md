# Science and learning guardrails

Reef Relay is an educational simulation, not a live sensor feed or species-identification instrument.

## Core idea

Coral reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor yet support about a quarter of marine species. In the experience they are described as **living cities and storm walls of the sea**: many organisms use their three-dimensional structure for shelter, feeding, reproduction, and coastline protection.

Primary background: [NOAA — coral reef ecosystems](https://www.noaa.gov/education/resource-collections/marine-life/coral-reef-ecosystems), [NOAA — value of coral reefs](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/coral_economy.html).

## What the interaction teaches

1. Observe form before naming a colony.
2. Treat a visual identification as “likely,” not certain; corallites, locality, and close examination can matter.
3. Connect skeletal architecture to habitat: branching and table forms create different light, flow, and shelter conditions.
4. Understand heat as accumulated stress over time.
5. Recognize bleaching as a severe warning, not automatic death.
6. See recovery as possible but conditional: clean water, herbivores, local protection, restoration, and climate action play different roles.

## Accuracy rules used in the app

- Healthy hard coral is often tan, brown, olive, or cream—not uniformly neon.
- Bleaching means coral has lost much of its symbiotic algae or their pigments. A bleached coral can still be alive, though it is energy-starved and at higher risk.
- Exposure statistics are not mortality statistics. “Reef area exposed to bleaching-level heat stress” must never be shortened to “reef killed.”
- La Niña does not guarantee cool, safe conditions everywhere; the Great Barrier Reef experienced mass bleaching during La Niña in 2022.
- Restoration can improve local resilience. It does not replace rapid emissions reduction or clean-water management.
- Scene temperatures, currents, positions, and health states are simulated unless a screen explicitly identifies a cited real-world observation.

Sources: [NOAA — what is coral bleaching?](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/coral_bleach.html), [NOAA Coral Reef Watch](https://coralreefwatch.noaa.gov/), [AIMS — Great Barrier Reef monitoring](https://www.aims.gov.au/monitoring-great-barrier-reef), [Singapore NParks — Sisters’ Islands Marine Park](https://www.nparks.gov.sg/sisters-islands-marine-park).

## Regional biome references

The Global Navigator terrains are simplified educational biomes, not exact site reconstructions.

- Great Barrier Reef: AIMS Long-Term Monitoring Program reports and bleaching updates inform the shelf terrain and mix of Acropora, Porites, Pocillopora, Seriatopora, Goniopora, and related coral forms.
- Sisters' Islands: NParks describes Sisters' Islands Marine Park as a conservation and research reef with coral nursery, seagrass, sponges, clams, seahorses, reef fish, turtles, and rays. Singapore reef studies also show turbid southern island reefs are often dominated by foliose, massive, sub-massive, and encrusting coral forms rather than branching cover.
- Coral Triangle: Coral Triangle Atlas and CTI-CFF biodiversity summaries inform the dense mixed reef, steep-wall, and high-fish-diversity treatment.
- Caribbean Reef: NOAA Fisheries and NOAA Ocean Service pages inform the spur-and-groove, elkhorn, staghorn, star coral, brain coral, Agaricia, and Porites educational profile.

Regional sources: [AIMS 2025-2026 GBR condition summary](https://www.aims.gov.au/monitoring-great-barrier-reef/gbr-condition-summary-2025-26), [AIMS GBR bleaching monitoring update](https://www.aims.gov.au/information-centre/news-and-stories/aims-gbr-bleaching-monitoring-update), [NParks Sisters' Islands Marine Park](https://www.nparks.gov.sg/visit/parks/park-detail/sisters-islands-marine-park/), [NParks coastal and marine ecosystems](https://www.nparks.gov.sg/nature/ecosystems/coastal-marine), [Singapore turbid reef community study](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep36260), [Coral Triangle Atlas](https://ctatlas.coraltriangleinitiative.org/About), [CTI-CFF Coral Triangle summary](https://new.coraltriangleinitiative.org/home-75-worlds-coral-species), [NOAA Fisheries staghorn coral](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/staghorn-coral), [NOAA Ocean Service brain coral](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/brain-coral.html).

## Child-centered design

The target audience is ages 8–13. The core loop is movement → observation → evidence → a short reveal. Voice is optional, text is brief, color is always paired with an icon/label, and nothing asks a learner for identifying information. Real swimming or diving always requires qualified local guidance and a responsible adult.
